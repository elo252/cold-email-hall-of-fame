# Cold Email Hall of Fame

A playbook distilled from 43 cold emails, calls and letters that got answers from people who receive thousands of messages. Every pattern below is backed by a real case in the casebook at the end. Use it to draft new emails, sharpen drafts, plan follow-ups and pick the right recipient.

The core insight, stated by several of the senders independently: **a cold email is a trade proposal, not a persuasion essay.** If the trade is good for the recipient and the email makes that obvious in ten seconds, it gets answered even when the writing is plain. If the trade is bad, no subject line saves it.

## Workflow

Follow these steps in order. Skip intake questions the user already answered.

### 1. Intake (ask only what's missing)

You need five things. Ask for the missing ones in a single short message, then proceed.

1. **Who** is the recipient, and what do they publicly care about right now?
2. **What** does the sender want? One thing.
3. **Why them?** Why is this recipient, specifically, the right person?
4. **Proof**: the sender's single strongest credential, metric or piece of work.
5. **Give**: what does the recipient get out of saying yes?

If the user can't answer #5, say so plainly and help them find one before drafting. Auren Hoffman's rule: if the recipient won't benefit, wait until you have something that benefits them.

### 2. Research the recipient (if tools allow)

If web search is available, spend a few searches on the recipient before writing. Look for:
- Recent public statements, posts, interviews, talks, launches, new funds or new companies (timing hooks)
- Pages where they say what they want ("I want to hear from X", "what I need help with")
- Their origin story or an unusual detail that's genuinely relevant
- How they prefer to be contacted, and whether they're known to answer cold email

Only use details that are public and professionally relevant. Never imply a relationship that doesn't exist.

### 3. Pick the pattern

Match the goal to a playbook below. Most good emails combine 3–4 tactics; don't stack all of them.

### 4. Draft

Write the email using the anatomy below. Then give the user:
- **2–3 subject line options** (different approaches, each labeled)
- **The email** (under 150 words unless it's an investor pitch with metrics, max ~200)
- **One follow-up** to send if there's no reply, adding something new
- **Two lines on why it should work**, citing the closest Hall of Fame case

### 5. Critique

Run the checklist on the draft (yours or the user's). Report failures specifically, with a rewritten line for each.

## Anatomy of a winning cold email

| Part | Job | Hall of Fame proof |
|---|---|---|
| **Subject** | Say exactly what this is. Clear beats clever, except when the clever line is earned by research. | Oshmyansky's "Cold pitch" (Cuban replied in ~5 min). Hagerman put the whole ask in the subject (reply in 7 min). Wessel's "fellow former water aerobics professional". |
| **Line 1: who + proof** | Prove in one sentence you're worth reading. A backer, metric, brand, byline or hard thing done. | Ghulati: "Google-backed", 30+ published papers. Talkdesk: led with ~$1M ARR. Seabrook: "the writer working on the New Yorker piece about you". |
| **Line 2–3: why you, why now** | The specific reason this recipient, at this moment. | Ghulati tied his pitch to Cuban's recent public comments on fake news. Andy wrote Kalanick as his new fund launched. Ozonian answered Cuban's own blog post asking for game developers. |
| **Value / proof of work** | What they get, or what you already made for them. | Lopuch put a free strategy in the email. Joseph demoed on Cuban's own company. Haldar filmed the video first. |
| **The ask** | One small, specific, easy-to-say-yes request. | "Can I send more?", "3 tips", "interview for my research paper", "30 min, here are two times". |
| **Sign-off** | Name, one-line title, link. No essay. | Hagerman's body was only his signature. |

Rules of thumb drawn from the cases:
- **Length**: most winners are under 150 words. Lemkin prefers 150–200 with two hard metrics for investor pitches.
- **Readable on a phone**, skimmable in 10 seconds. Cuban says he decides in a couple of seconds and deletes about 90%.
- **Never ask to "pick your brain", "hop on a quick call" or "grab coffee."** Tim Ferriss, Siddharth Haldar and Simar all flag these as reply-killers because they're vague requests for time.
- **A brief, honest acknowledgment of the cold approach is fine** ("Apologies for the cold message", "Cold pitch"). Grovelling is not.

## Playbooks by goal

### Funding (investors, angels, VCs)
Pattern: credibility-first → problem in plain words with real stakes → hard metrics, named customers → why this investor → tiny ask.
- Lead with your single best number or backer. Vague traction ("strong growth") reads worse than none.
- Problem before product (Janoch: compliance on spreadsheets, multi-million-dollar lawsuit risk).
- Ask for a call or permission to send more, never for money in email one. Offer two specific times.
- Choose recipients for a reason: Joseph emailed just the five best media investors; Levie emailed everyone. Both worked. Targeted beats volume when you have a clear thesis match; volume beats waiting.
- Expect hard questions back. Cuban answered Joseph with ~20 skeptical questions, then invested $3M. Answer every one directly.
- Cases: Ghulati, Janoch, Talkdesk, Levie, Hwang, Joseph, Ozonian, Oshmyansky.

### Job or internship
Pattern: show the work → no-risk offer → specific role or problem.
- Do part of the job before asking (Haldar shot content on spec; Lopuch gave free strategy).
- Arrive at a moment of need: new company, new team (Andy to Koller as Insitro launched).
- If rejected, reply with a lower-risk offer (Roy Lee: same hours, double effort, in the office until September). Haldar was rejected from Founders, Inc., then emailed his way in.
- Upgrade a weak signature with a legitimate role (Ferriss used his startup-association title).
- Cases: Roy Lee, Haldar, Ferriss, Sechrist, Andy/Koller, Bonatsos/Spiegel.

### Media: podcast guest, interview, quote
Pattern: who you write for → one precise question or small ask → their favorite topic.
- Make it answerable from the inbox (Hagerman: "3 tips" in the subject).
- Open on a subject they love talking about (Seabrook asked Gates what makes email unique).
- For big guests, persist with a new hook each time (Stebbings: 53 emails to Benioff over a year, varying whiskey, holiday spot, recent results).
- Cases: Seabrook, Hagerman, Stebbings, Corcoran/Kagan.

### Mentorship or advice
Pattern: meet or engage first → follow up with a specific question → act on the advice → report back.
- Never ask "will you be my mentor?" Ask for one piece of advice, act on it, report the result, repeat. That's how mentorships form (Haldar with Rian Gauvreau).
- In-person contact plus a sharp follow-up email converts well (Haldar at a university talk; Shezan bumping into Rajan Anandan at Starbucks).
- Cases: Haldar/Gauvreau, Shezan/Anandan, Jobs/Hewlett.

### Sales or partnership
Pattern: about them first → hint at the pain → offer a free piece of value → low-pressure ask.
- Open with their world, not your offer (Robinson: $10K/month retainer).
- Give a real idea in the email (Lopuch: customer-story strategy; grew her business 1,400%).
- Ask what's working for them (Berman: 17.5% booked meetings from 40 researched leads).
- Write the thing first (Andy published a full essay before pitching Packy McCormick).
- Cases: Berman, Robinson, Lopuch, Andy/McCormick, Sechrist.

### Access or a favor
Pattern: plain intro → concrete, small, urgent-if-true ask.
- Jobs, age 12: "I'm 12, I'm building a frequency counter, do you have spare parts?" Got parts and a summer job at HP.
- Simar asked an ambassador for a visa in 48 hours and got it in 36.
- Cases: Jobs/Hewlett, Simar, Bonatsos/Musk, Bridgewater/Pichai.

## Follow-ups

- Persistence works when each message adds something: a new result, a relevant link, a different angle. Stebbings needed 53; Ferriss emailed repeatedly but says he was never rude and never pushed the frequency.
- Default cadence: follow-up 1 after 4–7 days, then every 1–2 weeks, each with new information. Stop on a clear no, and respect any request to stop.
- A good follow-up is 1–3 sentences: the new thing, a one-line reminder of the ask.
- Warm the name first where you can (Corcoran made sure Kagan had seen his name before asking; a tweet reply before a DM reached Sam Parr).

## Critique checklist

Score each item pass/fail and fix every fail:

1. **Trade**: Is it obvious within ten seconds what the recipient gets?
2. **One ask**: Is there exactly one request, small and specific?
3. **First line**: Does it carry proof instead of pleasantries ("Hope you're well")?
4. **Why them**: Is there a specific, true reason this person was chosen?
5. **Length**: Under 150 words (200 for metric-heavy investor pitches)? Phone-readable?
6. **Subject**: Does it say what this is, or earn its cleverness with real research?
7. **Proof over claims**: Numbers, names, links, work samples instead of adjectives?
8. **Effort shown**: Is there anything they couldn't have received from anyone else?
9. **Easy yes**: Could they reply in one line? Are times or options offered?
10. **Tone**: Human, confident, low drama, no flattery, no guilt, no fake urgency?

Common failures to call out: talking about yourself for three paragraphs, multiple asks, "pick your brain", vague traction, generic compliments, attachments on first contact, mail-merge personalization ("I loved your recent post!"), and pretending to know someone.

## Guardrails

- Never fabricate credentials, metrics, mutual connections or prior contact. Every Hall of Fame email worked partly because its proof was real and checkable.
- Use only public, professionally relevant details for personalization. Don't dig into private life, family or home addresses.
- Persistence must add value and stop on a no. Don't help design harassment, guilt trips or deceptive urgency.
- For bulk sales outreach, remind the user about consent and anti-spam rules (CAN-SPAM, GDPR) where relevant.
- When citing a case to the user, say how well it's documented (see "Evidence" column). Treat self-reported and single-source stories as directional.
